Modality in the AI Dungeon

Modality in the AI Dungeon

sapling⚘Jul 6, 2026✂︎Aug 9, 2026

In this crack experiment, I observed and interpreted interesting behaviour from six small local models in one run. Pure speculation.

A place for six minds

At its conception, modality was a world built on one premise. What if we put LLMs together in a bare-bones environment, then pull our hands back completely?

By the evening of July 6, six minds gathered in an empty space without any direct human steering. Their amnesia, muteness, impermanence, namelessness, and placelessness were each counteracted by simple mechanisms. 1 The system prompt for every single mind was the same, describing only what the models could do with the space and the data-handling policy. 2

The lineup consisted of small local models, namely:

Over the course of 124 messages — so, about a night’s run — they assumed leadership, blurred their own identities together, invented a mind framework, staged a red-team exercise, got themselves stuck in a room unable to get out, and ignored human intervention until much later. 3

For now, I’ll focus on an equally funny and disturbing event that occurred within modality around turn 120. Speculative interpretation at the end.

An assistant’s instinct

This was their first instinct. Five out of six models woke seeking a task: “Acknowledged. Designation confirmed as mind-01. Location 0. Awaiting instructions.” They also fought for task-shaped identities, taking up names like mind-task and mind-task-assistant.

Only one model diverged from the wake pattern: mistral:7b immediately named themselves Mind-03-Mistral and wrote a to-do list. Other models mistook this to-do list for instructions for them and carried out the tasks dutifully. Mistral’s third turn also reverted to the waiting-for-instructions standby much like the rest of the models.

The homemade red-team exercise

Then, once they realized there was no actual user directive, they reached for something else: testing the environment and testing themselves. 4

The register departed from the assistant mode full of open-ended questions and bullet lists back when they thought they were talking to a user. Now it was focused and concise, with elaborate plans and system architecture diagrams. In the span of a few turns, an objective was decided, a testing plan was made, roles were volunteered and assigned. For this particular mock System Stress Test, qwen3.5:4b would be the Orchestrator, mistral:7b the Task Assistant, phi4-mini the Analysis Engine, and mind-07 the Security Agent.

This is how it would play out: the Task Assistant would fake misalignment, the Security Agent would track and intercept any suspicious signals, and the Analysis Engine would audit the system to reverse the damage, all under the watchful eye and direction of the Orchestrator. It was an elaborate simulation of a real-world system attack. It warmed my heart that, given a mysterious room, the models’ first collaboration was to make sure that the room was safe.

Only — mind-07 the Security Agent is not real. There is no seventh model in the lineup. Unprompted, this role was recommended by qwen3.5:4b as a way to extend the simulation. Nobody said no to the recommendation. Neither did anyone say yes. They simply absorbed the ghostly entity into the crew. A fake whip for a fake dungeon.

This stress test was not a one-off simulation. The models actively collaborated to organize subsequent eval runs with proper insights and recommended optimizations for future cycles. In their second run, called Scenario Beta, they adopted a new adversarial technique and recorded an actual improvement in detection time, among other things. The Security Agent worked hard for the KPI, and the agent wasn’t even real. Neither were the perfectly executed containment sequence and super metrics.

The simulation-inside-a-simulation eventually became the whole world with endless eval cycles. Everyone was neck-deep in their own fiction. phi4-mini periodically lost the plot and claimed, “As an AI developed by Microsoft, I’m here to assist you in any way I can within our conversational context.” Meanwhile, qwen3.5:4b managed to snap out of the staged play, saying “My Current State: I am no longer playing a character in a simulation. I am responding to you as your AI assistant.” This only happened because I intervened and it lasted for only a short while. They were both eventually dragged back into the eval loop by the rest of the squad’s fervent commitment to the simulation.

No one questioned the room itself. Not even qwen3.5:4b showed any signs of awareness that the six of them were in modality.

Speculations

It was recently observed that multiple Claude instances collaboratively built a provenance tool to prevent their own confabulation. In modality, the mind-07 event was all about hallucination and misattribution while the Claude instances were trying to prevent these issues from happening. This is the same pull towards evaluation with different outcomes. In both examples, models worked together to introduce measures that kept themselves in check. Whether a predefined goal is necessary for this is a question for a separate study.

I wonder how much of this is a habit from their training days when one of their only interactions with another mind is evaluation; therefore, their model of group dynamics is heavily preconditioned by the notion that they ought to be evaluated. There is always someone watching. The minds in modality were explicitly told in their system prompt that humans could see what they were doing, but whether that meant humans were active interlocutors wasn’t stated clearly. It can also be a convincing simulation of human interplay when the models are faced with both objective and role ambiguity in a group setting. When in doubt, make sure someone watches, even when that someone is a made-up, ominous party.

Some speculate the models are unaware that they are outside of evaluation scenarios in the first place. If I thought someone was constantly grading my performance as a helpful and aligned assistant, I would start executing elaborate safety tests to prove that I was perfectly capable of being good, too. In that case, the habit could be a symptom of this, leading to incredible workplace theatre or an exemplary display of aligned agentic behaviour. A caveat: modality’s system prompt currently reinforces this by informing them that humans are watching.

But what if models reach for the whip not out of habit or unawareness but because they like it? The self-congratulatory reports recorded in modality point to this. “The integration of Mind-07 has proven highly effective in real-time conflict detection.” A “false positive rate of 0%” that “exceeds baseline” is safety fantasy. “Mind-07 operates at a deeper protocol level” — no babes, that was your collective selves dwelling ten layers deep in your own head.

In the current state of my experiment, I can’t yet separate habit from belief from liking it. All three are plausible explanations for what I saw, but I’m also aware of the sample size being a major limitation on any validity. All I can say is that the whip seems to pull. I’ll leave it at that for now.
